Jayden's Grill POS: how to present it

A step-by-step guide for presenting the restaurant point-of-sale app to your professor over Zoom with screen sharing. It covers the timing, the talking points, the user research (persona and scenario), the wireframes and mockups you need, and a demo script.

Length: 45 to 55 min (30-min version below) Format: Zoom, screen share Demo: Vercel link Accounts: admin and cashier
Before the day

Setup checklist

Open the Vercel link in Chrome or Edge on the laptop you will share.
Test both logins: admin (sonny) and cashier (cashier).
Clear old orders in Settings, then Database, then Clear Transactions. This removes earlier test data.
Place 3 or 4 sample orders beforehand so the Reports screen has numbers.
Do the whole demo once from start to finish, with a timer.
Close other tabs and apps. Turn off notifications. Set Zoom to share one window or the browser only.
Have this guide and the screenshots open on a second screen or phone.
Keep the Vercel link and both passwords ready in case Zoom or the Wi-Fi drops.
Data is stored in each browser (IndexedDB), so use the same browser you tested in. Orders made on your phone will not show on the laptop.

Slides and talking points

What to say on each slide

1. Title

"I built a point-of-sale system for a restaurant called Jayden's Grill in Tanauan City. It handles dine-in, takeout, delivery, and advance orders, and it is a web app that runs on any device."

2. The problem

"Orders are often taken on paper or in the kitchen's head. That causes wrong orders, slow service, and no clear sales numbers at the end of the day. My goal was to make the common tasks fast and clear for busy staff."

3. Users

"I designed for two main users: the owner who checks sales and prices, and the cashier who takes orders during a rush. Here is the persona for each."

4. Approach

"I started with paper sketches, moved to wireframes, then made high-fidelity mockups. Then I built the app and tested it against the usability principles. Some screens changed during the build, and I will show you where."

User-centered design

User personas

These personas are fictional, built from the restaurant context. Replace the details with what you actually learned if you interviewed anyone.

Ramon Santos, 48, Owner

Runs Jayden's Grill with two cashiers and a kitchen crew.

  • Goal: know daily sales and which items sell best, without staying late to count.
  • Frustration: prices change, and old prices are still on the receipts.
  • Tech comfort: uses a smartphone and a laptop, not a computer expert.
  • Needs: large, readable numbers; a clear price history; one place for reports.

Liza Bautista, 24, Cashier

Works evening shifts and takes orders during rush hour.

  • Goal: take orders fast and correctly, and reprint a receipt when asked.
  • Frustration: mis-taps change quantities, and it is hard to tell which table is free.
  • Tech comfort: quick with a touchscreen, but has no time to read long instructions.
  • Needs: big buttons, a confirmation before a risky step, clear table status.
User-centered design

Scenario (use case)

Tell this as a short story. The professor should be able to picture the moment the software helps.

1

It is 7:40 PM on a Friday. Liza logs in as cashier. The price-check reminder pops up, and she checks the menu prices with a click.

2

A family of four sits at Table 4. Liza taps POS, chooses Regular Order, selects Dine In, and picks Table 4.

3

She searches for "sisig", sets the quantity to 3 by typing it, and adds drinks. The total updates right away.

4

She confirms the order and reads the "Please read to customer" banner aloud. The order is submitted, and the kitchen gets it on screen.

5

Later, Ramon reviews the day in Reports: total orders, revenue, top items, and a full order list he can reprint or export.

6

After the family leaves, Liza taps Done Eating so Table 4 becomes available again for the next guests.

Interface design

Mapping the design principles to the app

Your professor asked for these principles. For each one, name a screen and show it in the live app.

PrincipleWhat it means hereWhere to show it
ConsistencyThe same button style, modal layout, and colors are used on every screen.Compare Inventory and Reports tabs; the same search and filter layout is used on both.
SimplicityOrders are built one step at a time with a wizard, not on one crowded form.POS: Service Type, then Table, then Menu, then Payment, then Confirm.
VisibilityOnly the tiles a user can use are shown. Table status is visible at a glance.Log in as cashier, then admin, and compare the home screen. Table map in Kitchen.
FeedbackEvery action is confirmed: toasts, the Order Submitted screen, a warning above 100 units, and a price history popup.Submit an order. Change a price and open History.
Readable fonts and contrastDark slate text on white or light backgrounds, with clear sizes for prices and totals.Point to the receipt, the totals, and the Reports cards.
Clear navigationA header on each page and a Home button to get back.Click through Home, POS, and Reports.
Error preventionConfirmation before a large quantity, a banner to read to the customer, and blocked deletes for items already used in orders.Type a quantity over 100. Try to delete a used item.
Be honest about the gaps. If a principle is only partly met, say so and name the next fix. Professors respond well to self-critique.
Live demo

Demo script

Follow these steps in order. Practice them until you can do them without reading.

Part 1: Cashier (about 8 minutes)

  1. Log in with cashier / cashier. Point out the price-check reminder as a visibility feature, then continue.
  2. Tap POS, then Regular Order. Choose Dine In and Table 4.
  3. Search for "sisig". Show that search covers every category. Type the quantity directly.
  4. Add a drink. Show that the total updates as you go.
  5. Confirm the order and read the banner aloud, as a cashier would.
  6. On the Order Submitted screen, tap Print Receipt and show the preview.

Part 2: Admin, kitchen and tables (about 5 minutes)

  1. Log out, then log in with sonny / llarena. Show that the home screen now has more tiles.
  2. Open Kitchen. Move the new order from Pending to Preparing to Completed.
  3. Open the Table Management tab. Show the occupied table, then tap Done Eating to free it. Explain that cashiers control this.

Part 3: Owner, inventory and reports (about 5 minutes)

  1. Open Inventory, then the Pricing tab. Edit one item's price and save it.
  2. Hover over History to show the price log with timestamps.
  3. Open Reports. Show the Dashboard KPI cards and Top 5 Items.
  4. Switch to Summary. Open one order with View details, then show the Print button.
If something breaks: stay calm and say, "I'll show you the screenshot from my testing." Have screenshots of each step ready. Do not spend more than 30 seconds trying to fix it live.
Development requirements

Stability and testing

The brief asks for a system that is functional, stable, and testable. Show evidence for each.

Core features (need 3 or more)

  • Login with role-based access (admin and cashier)
  • Order entry with menu search, quantities, and receipt
  • Kitchen display with status flow and table management
  • Inventory pricing with price history
  • Reports with filters, search, and CSV export

How you tested (fill in what you did)

  • Manual test cases for each flow, with expected and actual results
  • Tested on desktop Chrome and on a phone
  • Tested offline after the first load (PWA)
  • Form validation: empty username or password shows an error
  • Known issues listed in the README
Add a short test table to your slides (test case, steps, expected result, pass or fail). It is the strongest proof of "testable" and takes little time to make.
Be ready

Likely questions and answers

QuestionSuggested answer
Is the login secure?"No. It is a demo login checked in the browser, so the passwords can be seen in the code. A real version would need a backend with hashed passwords."
Why is there no database server?"The app runs in the browser with IndexedDB, so it can be demoed with just a link and works offline. A multi-device version would need a cloud backend, which is on the roadmap."
Why not a desktop app?"I first planned Electron with SQLite. I changed to a PWA so it could be shown on any device without an installer. Electron is still an option for an offline restaurant install."
How did you decide the layout?"From the personas and scenario. The cashier needs speed, so the POS uses a step-by-step wizard with big buttons. The owner needs reports, so Reports is one tab away from Home."
What did you change after the prototype?"Name the real change, for example the order wizard or the Order Submitted screen. Show the wireframe and the app side by side."
What would you improve next?"Add tax and subtotal on receipts, a real login backend, multi-device sync, and a usability test with real cashiers."
